How to identify a vinyl pressing:
label, matrix and Discogs

Christophe · Record dealer since 2017 · Vinyl Run, Réunion Island · Lire en français

In a flea-market crate or at a record shop, the unwritten rule is never to look too interested. But there is a far more useful skill than a poker face: reading a record in ten seconds. The label, the matrix, a few codes scratched into the run-out groove: it is all there, printed or etched, for anyone who can decode it. This guide gives you the keys.

The label: a record's passport

The center label holds more information than it seems. Color, logo, typeface, catalog number: every detail can tell you about the era, the country and the importance of a pressing.

Take Columbia in the US. Original pressings from the late 1950s to 1962 carry the famous "six eye" label, with three small eye logos on each side of the spindle hole. It then gives way to the "two eye" label with its "360 Sound" banner, and to simpler designs in the 1970s. Spotting those variations on a Miles Davis or Bob Dylan album tells you at once whether you hold an early pressing or a reissue. The same logic applies to Parlophone in the UK, to Island Records, whose label colors and designs changed from one period to the next, or to Barclay and Pathé Marconi for French pressings.

The one line to remember: color + logo + catalog number = manufacturing period. Most major labels are documented on Discogs with their label variants, a precious resource to check before you buy.

The matrix: the code etched in the silence

Flip the record over. In the run-out groove (the smooth area between the last groove and the label), you will find inscriptions, hand-etched or machine-stamped. This is the matrix, and it is where the cutting and plating engineers left their marks. Each stamper leaves its own signature in the vinyl.

First comes the side's code, usually the catalog number followed by a letter or figure that tracks the lacquer or stamper generation (1A, 1B, 2A, or -1, -2 on many UK pressings; conventions vary by label and country). As a rule, the lowest numbers are the earliest cuts, the ones collectors look for. A higher number usually means a later lacquer cut for a later run. It is not always worse, but the difference can sometimes be heard, depending on the record's condition and your system.

Next come the marks that identify the pressing plant. Collectors have catalogued these codes plant by plant, on Discogs and on specialist wikis, so a quick search usually tells you where and roughly when a record was pressed. The matrix is also what helps us authenticate Réunion séga and maloya pressings, many of which are missing from Discogs altogether.

And sometimes a set of hand-etched initials changes everything. "RL" is the signature of Robert Ludwig, one of the reference mastering engineers of the era: his "RL" cut of Led Zeppelin II is among the most sought-after pressings of that album. Some engineers also left messages. "A Porky Prime Cut" and "Pecko Duck" were etched by George "Porky" Peckham, a British cutting engineer, on countless UK records. They are not defects: they are marks of authenticity that collectors have catalogued for decades.

First pressing, repress, promo copy: the real differences

A first pressing is a copy made for an album's original release, from the earliest stampers. But "first pressing" does not automatically mean "best sound". The physical condition of a record always matters more than its generation.

Later pressings may use later stampers, thinner vinyl or a different compound. On some in-demand albums the difference is audible. On others, a 1980s Japanese repress easily beats the original 1973 US pressing, because the plant was better, the vinyl cleaner and quality control stricter.

Promo copies deserve a special mention. You recognize them by a white label or a "Promotional Copy, Not for Sale" stamp. They were sent to radio stations and journalists before the official release. Their appeal: pressed early, often with care, in limited numbers. Their downside: radio stations in the 1970s were not kind to their styluses, and condition can be terrible. (A cut corner or a drill hole in the sleeve, by the way, usually marks deleted stock sold off cheap, not a promo.)

Japanese pressings are a category of their own. Known for strict plants, clean vinyl and careful packaging, often with an obi strip (the paper band wrapped around the sleeve), they are considered by many collectors the best available versions of a large number of rock, soul and jazz albums, often at friendlier prices than US or UK originals.

Discogs prices: reading them without getting fooled

Discogs is the world's vinyl encyclopedia, with a marketplace attached. Reading a listing properly takes a little practice.

First, condition. The grading scale runs from M (Mint) down to G (Good) and below. In practice, most interesting used records sit between VG+ (Very Good Plus: light signs of play, near-perfect sound) and VG (Very Good: some surface noise, but very playable). A VG+ copy at €15 is usually worth it. A VG at €25 with scratches visible under the light, much less so.

The classic trap: sellers overgrade by one step. What they call NM (Near Mint) is often a decent VG+, and their VG+ is sometimes an honest VG. When you buy online, always ask for photos of the matrix and of the surface under raking light: a torch held at a low angle reveals everything.

Second, the prices shown on Discogs are not real prices: they are asking prices. What counts is the sales history ("Last Sold", median price). A seller can ask €80 for a pressing that usually sells for €22. The history gives you the real market.

Finally, a price guide is a tool, not the truth. One sale on the other side of the world does not set the value of a worn copy at a flea market. This is especially true for Indian Ocean pressings (Madagascar, Mauritius, Réunion, Comoros), where a Discogs entry may only have a handful of sales.
